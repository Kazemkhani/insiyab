Why does the evening
fall behind?

Dubai’s bus records show much more lateness in the evening. When a trip overruns its timetable and the break before the next trip, the delay carries forward. Insiyab tests a trip-time forecast and gives planners a measured list of routes and hours that need attention. A separate control-room drill shows how AI can gather updates for a human decision.

The problem

The timetable said 19:23.
The bus left at 19:36.

Someone at the stop planned their evening around 19:23. This illustration is about the plan, not a judgement about a driver. The bus was still finishing its last trip, because that trip took 96 minutes and the timetable gave it 67.

When a trip takes longer than its scheduled time and the break between trips, the same bus cannot start its next trip on time. Insiyab shows the planner where the records reveal this pressure.

Who needs to know?

On the recorded day, 696 linked departures were due before their bus returned from the previous trip. Of those, 85% then started more than five minutes late. The busiest hour for this flag was 19:00.

Measured by lane 3 from the runtime records, 15 December 2025. This is a different denominator from the 589 of 945 late-start figure above.

In our drill, four team members provide the missing operational facts. The AI briefs Amir, acting as duty manager. He chooses an option; the AI relays his choice and records each acknowledgement.

  1. Report. Driver: ability to continue. Depot: spare availability. Station: passenger information. Planner: what the evidence can support.
  2. Decide. The duty manager weighs the reports and chooses, or takes no action.
  3. Relay. Staff hear the manager’s decision and confirm receipt. Missing answers stay visible.

Where it helps, hour by hour.

RTA's timetableThe model

The timetable’s time covers 29 in 100 evening-peak trips; the planning standard is 85. The worklist uses measured trip times to show this gap.

The planner’s list and the control-room flag are measured from recorded trips. They do not use the forecast’s output. The pitch split groups trips by scheduled departure; some training trips may still have been running at the cutoff. The stricter test below uses only finished trips.

What Mustafo’s independent Dataiku build found

Project John, 18 November 2026. These are separate runs from the original INSIYAB result above. On the same 2,043 evening-peak trips, the built-in model misses by 7.15 minutes, about 13% closer than the timetable. The tuned script misses by 6.93 minutes, about 16% closer.

In the current strict next-hour test, trees miss by 7.76 minutes, the simple rule by 7.48, and the linear model by 7.37, against 8.25 for the timetable. Trees improve by about 6%; earlier runs gave 8%. The simpler methods win this harder test.

In the scheduled-departure pitch test (A), from 19:00 the current trees miss by 5.06 minutes against 4.40 for the timetable.

The work is in John, not yet copied into INSIYAB. Read the chain, definitions and independent checks.

Only 6% past the simple rule.

Who uses it

A planner.
Every timetable change.

RTA changes timetables every one to two months and routes four times a year. Each night adds a day of records. Before each change, a planner opens the worklist, sees where the evening runs long, and decides how much time to give it. Boardings, never people "waiting". Nothing about one card, one driver or one vehicle is shown.

Give the evening
its time.

About 244 bus hours of extra running time from 16:00 to 18:59 is the measured planning shortfall at the 85th percentile. It is not a model-produced saving or a guarantee for another day. About 630 bus hours across the whole day.
