Six controllers.
1,400 buses.

Insiyab sees it coming. The controller decides. The signal is in the data before 19:23: the bus on route 310 is still out on its previous trip, which took 96 minutes when the timetable gave it 67. When this happens, the next departure leaves late 85 times in 100. Insiyab reads that signal, forecasts how long each evening trip will really take, flags which buses may overflow, which stops are in the heat, and suggests a fix early. When a critical alert is not acknowledged, it calls driver, depot, station and planning at once and gives the controller one briefing with three options. A person always decides.

The problem

The timetable said 19:23.
The bus left at 19:36.

Someone at the stop planned their evening around 19:23. This illustration is about the plan, not a judgement about a driver. The bus was still finishing its last trip, because that trip took 96 minutes and the timetable gave it 67.

Our answer

Insiyab sees it coming.
The controller decides.

Insiyab watches the evening before it happens. It predicts which buses will run late and which stops are in the heat, and suggests a fix early. When a critical alert is not acknowledged, it makes the calls and gives the controller one short briefing. A person always decides.

Running latemeasured model

Insiyab watches every departure. When the bus is still out on its previous trip, the next departure starts late 85 in 100 times (696 such departures, measured). Its trip-time forecast is 15% closer than the timetable on 2,043 evening trips it never saw.

Source: RTA records, 15 Dec 2025, analysed in Dataiku (opendata/onday/HOW-IT-CONNECTS.md)

Heatpriority rule

The same delay matters more at a bare pole in the sun. Among the 1,600 stops whose kind is published, there are 993 bare poles and 553 air-conditioned shelters. Insiyab ranks delay higher at poles, especially at midday and in summer.

RTA 2020 to 2022 reports; opendata/public/bus-stops.csv

Lanes and roadsrule

Bus lanes can cut peak journey times by up to 59% on routes that use them. When a route gains a bus lane, Insiyab flags its forecast for review: a model trained before the change is wrong after it.

Khaleej Times, 21 June 2026

Eventsrule

When a public event ends near a bus route, Insiyab checks the calendar and flags the affected routes ahead of the crowd, so the controller can decide before the surge arrives.

RTA event services; list and rule, not a model

Connections and last mileproposal

When a bus is predicted late, Insiyab can flag the connection its riders are heading for and suggest a hold or a Bus On Demand minibus. Bus On Demand carried 984,929 riders in 2025, up 136%, across 17 areas.

From the problem to the calls

The bus is still out.
Now someone has to phone around.

On the recorded day, 696 linked departures were due before their bus returned from the previous trip. Of those, 85% then started more than five minutes late. The busiest hour for this flag was 19:00.

Measured by lane 3 from the runtime records, 15 December 2025. This is a different denominator from the 589 of 945 late-start figure above.

That is the moment a controller starts phoning: the driver, the depot, the station. RTA told us six controllers oversee about 1,400 buses a shift, so every call is time taken from the decision. Insiyab makes those calls at once, gives the controller one short briefing with three options, and relays the choice. It never chooses.

Twenty-second illustration. A drill on our own phones, not an RTA system; every report in it is simulated.

  1. Report. Insiyab asks the driver, depot, station and planning desk at the same time. Missing or unsure answers stay marked as such.
  2. Brief and decide. The controller gets one briefing with three options and chooses. In the pilot, each option also shows its likely effect from the trip-time forecast.
  3. Relay. Insiyab tells each person the decision and records "Message received". Received means told, not done.

The decision, with its price

Three options.
Each one priced, each with a name on it.

Route 310 is 22 minutes late and the next bus is 20 minutes away. The call gives the duty manager three options. Each says what it does to passenger waiting, what it costs, and who must approve it.

OPTION A

Send the standby bus

63.6% less waiting
Who it helps
About 213 passengers wait 8 minutes instead of 22.
What it costs
One standby bus and driver, for one trip.
Who decides
Depot controller
OPTION B

Hold the next departure two minutes

9.1% more waiting
Who it helps
Passengers further along, if two buses would have bunched. Not priced: the data holds no stop-by-stop gaps.
What it costs
The 213 passengers on the next bus wait 2 minutes longer.
Who decides
Station supervisor
OPTION C

Take no action

The baseline
Who it helps
Nobody now. 4,686 passenger-minutes of waiting.
What it costs
No waiting is added or removed.
Who decides
Duty manager

How it speeds the decision. One call gives the manager all three options with their figures. The person who must approve the chosen option is called next, told it is theirs to carry out, and confirms. Every step is logged.

SIMULATED DRILL. Passengers affected = 640 boardings this hour x 20 / 60 = 213. Waiting = passengers x extra minutes each, compared with doing nothing. Assumes every affected passenger arrived for the planned time, and that a standby bus is 8 minutes away: invented for the drill, not confirmed by any staff report. We have not measured how many minutes this saves a real control centre. The tool advises; a named person decides. Nothing is sent to RTA.

Where it helps, hour by hour.

RTA's timetableThe model

The timetable’s time covers 29 in 100 evening-peak trips; the planning standard is 85. The worklist uses measured trip times to show this gap.

The planner’s list and the control-room flag are measured from recorded trips. They do not use the forecast’s output. The pitch split groups trips by scheduled departure; some training trips may still have been running at the cutoff. The stricter test below uses only finished trips.

What Mustafo’s independent Dataiku build found

Project John, 18 November 2026. These are separate runs from the original INSIYAB result above. On the same 2,043 evening-peak trips, the built-in model misses by 7.15 minutes, about 13% closer than the timetable. The tuned script misses by 6.93 minutes, about 16% closer.

In the current strict next-hour test, trees miss by 7.76 minutes, the simple rule by 7.48, and the linear model by 7.37, against 8.25 for the timetable. Trees improve by about 6%; earlier runs gave 8%. The simpler methods win this harder test.

In the scheduled-departure pitch test (A), from 19:00 the current trees miss by 5.06 minutes against 4.40 for the timetable.

The work is in John, not yet copied into INSIYAB. Read the chain, definitions and independent checks.

Who uses it

The control centre.
And the planners behind it.

On the day: the duty controller gets one briefing instead of many phone calls, and decides. This week: the event team plans the small events nobody has time for today. Each timetable change, every one to two months: the planner opens the worklist and decides how much time each route and hour needs. Boardings, never people "waiting". Nothing about one card, one driver or one vehicle is shown.
